## Week 1 — Proof, and the enemy named

### Day 01 · Mon 2026-09-14 · LinkedIn

**The $1.2M campaign that ended in an acquisition**

*Wins · Long text post*

**Hooks**

A. A private aviation brokerage I wrote cold email for was acquired by V2Jets last month.
B. I wrote cold emails for a private jet brokerage. The thing I am proudest of is that every dollar traces back to a specific send.
C. $1.2M in ARR and $7.4M in pipeline for one private aviation brokerage, and an acquisition at the end of it.

**Script**

A private aviation brokerage I wrote cold email for was acquired by V2Jets last month. Before that, the campaign I ran for them generated $1.2M in ARR and $7.4M in pipeline that we could trace back, line by line, to specific sends.

I am posting this because most agency case studies stop at the part where the client is happy, and the only number that survives a due diligence process is the one a CFO can find.

Three things carried that campaign, and none of them are a tool you can buy.

The first is that selling a charter flight means selling a decision someone makes under time pressure with a number attached that would make most people flinch, so the email could never open by explaining who we were. It opened on the specific thing about that prospect's travel that made their current arrangement expensive, which meant research happened before writing, every time, per prospect.

The second is that we wrote to loss. A broker telling you about a better experience is standing in a line of brokers telling you the same thing, while a broker holding a number for what your current setup quietly costs you every quarter is holding something you have to answer.

The third is boring and carried more weight than the other two combined. We kept sending. Most of the replies landed well past the first message, and every operator who quit at touch two never found out what was waiting at touch six.

None of that needs a bigger team. It needs research that does not scale, which is exactly why most agencies will not do it, and exactly why the ones who will end up with results that hold up when someone acquires the company.

Outbound that cannot be traced to revenue is activity wearing a nicer name.

**CTA**

If you want to know what this looks like against your list, my DMs are open.

**Levers:** Authority (third-party acquisition), Loss aversion, Quantitative specificity, Familiarity bias (touch count)

### Day 05 · Fri 2026-09-18 · YouTube

**The cold email campaign behind an acquisition**

*Wins · Talking head + screen recording, 9-11 min*

> ⚠︎ **Verify before posting.** The 18-out-of-20 loss aversion ratio is UNVERIFIED per the vault. Script says 'most of the time' instead. Do not restore the ratio unless the test data is confirmed.

**Hooks**

A. A company I wrote cold email for got acquired. I am going to show you the actual campaign.
B. $7.4M in pipeline from cold email, in an industry where everybody says cold email does not work.
C. This is the campaign I point to when somebody asks me whether any of this is real.

**Script**

[0:00 COLD OPEN] A private aviation brokerage I wrote cold email for was acquired by V2Jets in August. Before that, the campaign generated $1.2M in ARR and $7.4M in pipeline. I am going to walk you through what the emails actually said and why, because the case study version of this story is useless to you and the mechanics are not.

[0:30 THE SETUP] Private aviation is a market where everyone assumes cold outreach is dead, and the assumption is reasonable, because the people being written to are wealthy, busy, heavily gatekept, and receive a genuinely absurd volume of solicitation. Every broker in the category writes the same email, which opens by introducing the company and then describes a superior experience. That email is invisible. It is invisible because the prospect has read it forty times from forty companies and has no way to tell any of them apart.

[1:45 THE FIRST DECISION — RESEARCH BEFORE WRITING] The first thing we changed is the order of operations. Most teams write a template and then look for people to send it to. We looked at a person, found the specific thing about how they were currently flying that was costing them money or time, and wrote the email second. That is slower per email by a factor of ten, and it is the entire reason the campaign worked.

I want to be precise about what research means here, because people hear research and think they need a data tool. It meant knowing which routes someone was flying repeatedly, what that pattern costs on a charter-by-charter basis against a different arrangement, and what specifically about their situation made the current setup a bad fit. All of that is public or inferable if you are willing to sit with it for fifteen minutes.

[3:30 THE SECOND DECISION — WRITE TO LOSS] People buy because they are scared of losing something, far more reliably than because they want something. I have tested gain framing against loss framing across a lot of campaigns and loss wins most of the time.

Applied here, that means the email never said we can give you a better flying experience. It said, roughly, you are flying this route this often, at this structure, and here is what that arrangement is costing you every quarter that you would not be spending under a different one. That sentence has a number in it, and a number is something a person has to respond to internally even if they never reply.

There is a line people cross with loss framing where it turns manipulative, and the line is honesty. If the cost you are naming is real and you can show your math, you are informing someone. If you invented it to create pressure, you are lying to a rich person who has been lied to professionally for thirty years and will spot it instantly.

[5:45 THE THIRD DECISION — KEEP SENDING] The least interesting part of this carried more weight than either of the first two. Most of the replies came after the sixth touch. Not the first, not the second. Six or more.

This is the part almost nobody executes, because touch two feels like enough, touch four feels rude, and touch six feels insane. Familiarity is doing the work there. Somebody who has seen your name six times over a few weeks reads the seventh message differently than a stranger reads the first, and it has almost nothing to do with the copy getting better.

[7:15 WHAT I WOULD DO DIFFERENTLY] We over-indexed on volume early. The first stretch of the campaign, I was still trying to get reach up, and the reply rate on that stretch was noticeably worse than the stretch where we cut the list and tripled the research time per prospect. I lost a few weeks learning that the smaller list wins, and I would skip that now.

[8:30 THE PART THAT MATTERS TO YOU] Everything I just described is available to anyone. There is no tool in this story. The reason most agencies will not do it is that it does not scale cleanly, it cannot be handed to a junior person on day one, and it makes the weekly report look thin next to a team that sent forty thousand emails.

Then the quarter closes, and one of those two teams can point at revenue.

[9:30 CLOSE] The company got acquired. I do not get to claim credit for an acquisition, and I am not going to. What I will claim is that when the buyers looked at where the pipeline came from, it traced to something specific, and that is the only standard I care about being held to.

**CTA**

[0:45 WHAT THE WORK ACTUALLY WAS] Strip away what the phrase sounds like. The work was sitting with someone who wanted a specific outcome, watching them attempt it and fail, and diagnosing why in real time. Not theory. A person tries something in front of you, it does not land, and you have to identify which part of the approach broke, and you get corrected immediately and publicly when you are wrong about it.
